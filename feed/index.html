abstract

ABSTRACT Reconstructions of past permafrost have most frequently utilized relict landforms and sedimentary structures genetically related to the growth and decay of permafrost. However, the majority dated so far formed during the Last Glacial cycle, while older ones are much less common and have mostly been dated using purely relative and/or numerically aided stratigraphic methods. Here, we describe the stratigraphic position, morphology, internal structures and optically‐stimulated‐luminescence (OSL) ages of a rare Middle Pleistocene frost wedge recovered in a sedimentary section in Dolní Chabry, Czech Republic (50.14171° N, 14.44964° E, 302 m above sea level). As such, the OSL dates are unique and valuable, which is further enhanced by their exceptionally old ages.

abstract

The Dinaric Mountains are a mountain range stretching NW–SE from the Alps to the Albanides–Hellenides in central Europe. The highest parts were occupied by small ice fields and/or valley glaciers during the Quaternary cold periods, while even today some small glaciers still exist in topoclimatically favourable areas. The northernmost part of the Dinaric Mountains is called Trnovski Gozd (1495 m a.s.l.). It is a high karst plateau characterized by conical hills, uvalas, solution dolines, and numerous speleological features. In addition to the karst, Trnovski Gozd also has a palaeoglacial landscape above 1100 m a.s.l. Here, we present the glacial history of Trnovski Gozd, which was studied using geomorphological mapping, terrestrial cosmogenic nuclide (TCN, 36 Cl) dating, and the Parallel Ice Sheet Modelling (PISM). The glacial evidence on the Trnovski Gozd is very scattered and sparse. Geomorphological mapping suggests a glaciated area of ~6 km 2 . Five samples from boulders located on the crest of the largest moraine in the area were dated. The results yield a median age of 19.5±2.1 ka (corrected for a denudation rate of 20 mm ka −1 ) and are consistent with ages of the global Last Glacial Maximum (LGM). None of the Parallel Ice Sheet Model (PISM) simulations fully reproduce the geomorphologically reconstructed glacier extent. However, a scenario employing a temperature offset of −7.0 °C and a precipitation scaling factor of 0.95 relative to modern values provide the best fit, yielding a glaciated area of 27.4 km 2 while successfully reproducing the position of the dated moraine. The Equilibrium Line Altitude (ELA) derived from the simulation results equals 1246 m a.s.l. and is similar to the LGM ELAs of some nearby isolated alpine glaciers. The temperature and precipitation offsets are also consistent with those observed across the Alps during the LGM. Reconstructions of isolated glaciers near the Alpine ice stream network provide valuable benchmarks for evaluating and refining palaeoclimate models.

abstract

Petermann Glacier (PG), the largest marine-terminating glacier in northern Greenland based on catchment area and ice discharge, plays a key role in modulating ice export from the Greenland Ice Sheet (GrIS). With an upstream catchment connected to the GrIS interior via a deep subglacial canyon, its future stability has major implications for sea level rise. This review synthesizes recent advances in understanding PG’s dynamics across three critical interfaces. At the surface, reanalysis data and regional climate models diverge from observations in estimates of air temperature and surface mass balance, underscoring the need for improved in situ data and models. At the ocean boundary, enhanced basal melting driven by subglacial runoff and Atlantic water intrusions is identified as dominant driver of recent mass loss of PG, with continued warming posing a serious threat to the stability of the floating tongue. At the bed, geophysical synthesis reveals complex geology, likely spatial variability in geothermal heat flux, and the megacanyon’s influence on seasonal hydrology and velocity fluctuations. An ongoing rifting event, initiated in September 2025, highlights PG’s vulnerability. The anticipated retreat reduces buttressing and brings the terminus closer to the grounding zone, where additional calving could trigger retreat and accelerate ice discharge. As one of Greenland’s largest floating ice tongues, PG may provide important insights into the stability of other marine-terminating glaciers across the GrIS. Improved observations, sustained monitoring of oceanographic and atmospheric parameters, and high-resolution modeling are critical for constraining projections of PG’s future and its role in GrIS stability.

abstract

Reconstructing Antarctic Ice Sheet evolution through the last deglaciation is crucial for quantifying global sea level rise and solid Earth loading changes. This endeavor requires both empirical geologic observations and numerical ice sheet models: geologic data offer valuable but spatially and temporally fragmented snapshots of past glacial configurations, whereas numerical models provide three-dimensional physically based ice evolution but generate a wide range of plausible deglacial histories. Together, these approaches enable process-level insights into ice sheet sensitivity to warmth. Here we focus on the marine seafloor, combining geologic information gleaned from sediment cores and mapped seafloor landforms with numerical ice sheet modeling. We develop model-data comparison metrics that assess both the timing of grounding-line recession and its spatial pattern across the Amundsen and Ross Sea continental shelves. The application of these metrics to a targeted ensemble of ice sheet model simulations demonstrates the power of geologic data to constrain the chronology and amplitude of modeled deglacial ice loss. Marine radiocarbon and seafloor landform observations are translated into glaciologically consistent reconstructions of regional ice sheet deglaciation, lending quantitative context for the reconstruction of low-slope ice sheets that remain pinned on seafloor highs as deglaciation progresses. This work highlights the use of physically self-consistent ice sheet models as a powerful tool to contextualize geologic records of Antarctic deglaciation.

abstract

Abstract This study presents an annually laminated sediment record from the infilled maar lake at Hoher List in the Eifel volcanic field (Germany), documenting the “Last Glacial Inception” (LGI) at the end of the Eemian interglacial. The landscape of the late Eemian was dominated by a mixed forest containing Abies alba (silver fir). The collapse of this forest occurred within only a few years and was accompanied by frequent wildfires, aeolian dust deposition, a decline of all thermophilous tree species, and the disappearance of Abies , all indicating increasing aridity. The transition unfolded in three distinct phases. LEAP-I (Late Eemian Aridity Pulse I) lasted approximately 250 years and was followed by a 750-year recovery phase during which the interglacial forest returned. LEAP-II was characterized by comparable arid conditions but persisted for at least 750 years, followed by a 350-year phase of boreal forest development. The final transition to stadial conditions occurred during LEAP-III, marked by a pronounced lake-level decline, expansion of grassland, scattered birch and pine, and reduced summer temperatures, likely corresponding to North Atlantic cold event C25. The LGI lasted at least 2700 years, whereas individual aridification pulses developed within decades, indicating rapid aridification events superimposed on a long-term cooling trend.

The AlpIce‐1 database is the first compilation of published geomorphological and geochronological markers constraining build‐up, culmination and decay of glaciers from the Last Glacial Maximum through the Holocene across the entire European Alps. AlpIce‐1 integrates more than 4200 surface‐exposure and radiocarbon ages, homogenized, quality controlled and evaluated for their palaeoglaciological context, compiled from 268 peer‐reviewed publications and over 1300 sites across the European Alps. The database includes ages originally obtained from a wide range of landforms and deposits including moraine ridges, lakes, mires, sediment outcrops, ice cores, bedrock surfaces, caves and archaeological sites. AlpIce‐1 is designed as an open‐access resource supporting both field and modelling studies across the Alps and is intended for all communities interested in the late Quaternary. In light of emerging next‐generation numerical ice models, AlpIce‐1 offers an extensive and robust empirical basis for evaluating future (palaeo)glacier simulations.

abstract

Surface albedo is crucial for the polar energy balance. Blowing snow (BLSN) frequently perturbs coastal Antarctic albedo, yet its specific radiative effects and observability by medium-resolution satellites remain poorly understood. This study combines multi-source ground observations at Zhongshan Station with Sentinel-2 and MODIS imagery to evaluate the multi-scale albedo response to BLSN. The results show that when snowfall and BLSN occur simultaneously, they generally lead to spatially uniform surface brightening. Conversely, when BLSN occurs after snowfall has ended, strong winds drive snow erosion and redistribution, producing highly dispersed, interleaved patches of local positive and negative albedo changes. Scale-bridging analysis reveals that spatially uniform brightening is reliably captured by medium-resolution satellites. However, these highly dispersed local signals extensively cancel each other out during spatial averaging. This study suggests that the observability of dynamic surface perturbations depends on their sub-pixel spatial structure. Consequently, a near-zero signal at medium resolutions does not guarantee an unchanged surface, highlighting a hidden cancellation effect that obscures critical local energy redistributions.
