The last glacial cycle in western Norway—extensive MIS 5d glaciation, multiple glacial advances and LGM glacial advance phases

Eiliv A. Larsen, Hans Petter Sejrup, BRENT C. WARD, William E. N. Austin, Hilary H. Birks, Oddvar Longva, … (+4)
Boreas · 2026-10-22
53.0 matched: ice sheet, ice flow, glacier, deglaciation, last glacial maximum, LGM, glacial cycle, MIS stage, foraminifera, radiocarbon, sea level
abstract

We have investigated fine‐grained marine sediments buried under delta sediments and multiple till beds in a tributary valley in western Norway. The marine bed contains bivalves, foraminifera and terrestrial plant material. The fauna and flora are rather poor, but the presence of molluscan species Mytilus edulis and Arctica islandica suggests that Atlantic waters reached the coastal areas at the time of deposition. The age range of radiocarbon dates from about 25 to 50 ka BP conflicts with previous studies from the area and is considered as minimum ages only. This conclusion is supported by amino acid epimerization values in molluscs from the radiocarbon‐dated unit, indicating that it should be correlated with an Early Weichselian, MIS 5c interstadial. The marine unit and overlying delta succession are situated on the outer coast up to 56 m above present sea level, suggesting that they were deposited during a high sea‐level stand following retreat of an extensive MIS 5d glaciation. A compiled Weichselian glaciation curve shows five major glacial advances extending to the outermost coast or beyond. These advances seem to correspond to periods with North Atlantic sea surface cooling. Three till units on top of the delta are interpreted to represent glacier advance prior to the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) and during the subsequent deglaciation. Clast fabrics in the lower till and orientation of clastic dikes show an upvalley component opposite to LGM maximum ice‐flow direction. This indicates that initial glacier advance was by fjord glaciers extending to the outer coast while there was limited glacier extent in coastal mountain areas. This interpretation favours fast ice flow along the fjords and major supply of ice from interior mountain areas. Glacier growth in coastal mountains led to merging with fjord glaciers, reorientation of the ice‐flow direction and ultimately maximum glacier extent. Empirical data on ice‐sheet growth are extremely rare and may have been overlooked in many stratigraphic records. Such data are vital for understanding behaviour of ice sheets and in a wider sense, the climate system.

Reversible Atlantic overturning despite continued Greenland ice sheet melt

Chuncheng Guo, Shuting Yang, Ilana Schiller‐Weiss, Jorge A. Bernales, Steffen M. Olsen, Torben Koenigk, … (+3)
Science Advances · 2026-10-14 · cites-me new
50.0 matched: ice sheet, Greenland, meltwater, mass loss, overshoot, irreversible, Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, sea level, sea-level rise, Earth system model, Earth system, global warming
abstract

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is considered vulnerable to irreversible weakening or collapse under global warming and related Greenland ice sheet (GrIS) melt, yet its resilience to either forcing remains poorly constrained. Here, we use a carbon dioxide emission-driven Earth system model with an interactive GrIS to assess AMOC reversibility under a range of idealized carbon dioxide emission pathways that produce near-linear global warming up to 10 kelvins (K), stabilization across 1.5 to 9 K, and subsequent cooling. We find that although the AMOC reaches threshold-defined collapsed states, these do not represent dynamical tipping in this model: The overturning weakens quasi-linearly with global mean temperature increase yet consistently and promptly recovers under cooling. In contrast, GrIS mass loss accelerates with warming, continues through stabilizations, and is only slowed by cooling, committing the planet to long-term sea level rise. These results reveal contrasting resilience across climate system components: Under transient carbon dioxide forcing, AMOC strength remains dynamically reversible despite continued Greenland meltwater input, whereas the GrIS is locked into persistent decline. Our findings underscore the urgency of rapid emission cuts to limit climate overshoot, AMOC weakening, and irreversible ice sheet loss.

abstract

During interglaciations, the Laurentide Ice Sheet (LIS), which is the largest of the Pleistocene ice sheets, retreats to Arctic Canada, where it achieves a minimum configuration. The reexpansion of the LIS following this minimum marks the start of the next glaciation. Here, we show that the last remaining remnant of the most recent LIS, Barnes Ice Cap (Baffin Island, Canada), achieved its minimum interglacial dimensions after ~3.6 thousand years ago, before reexpanding ~2000 years ago, marking the onset of the next glaciation. The new LIS reached maximum dimensions in ~1960 CE, after which anthropogenic summer warming resulted in a negative mass balance, ice margin recession, and the termination of the next glaciation. The demise of the next glaciation, despite the ongoing decline in summer insolation, illustrates the influence of anomalous greenhouse gas forcing on climate.

Environmental dynamics and timing of the late Eemian interglacial to the Weichselian pleniglacial at Lichtenberg, Germany: implications for the Oerel and Glinde interstadials

Alexis Badai Samudra, Michael Hein, Martin Theuerkauf, Neda Rahimzadeh, Marcel Weiss, David Colin Tanner, … (+1)
Boreas · 2026-10-19
35.0 matched: Greenland, interglacial, Eemian, MIS stage, proxy, sediment core, radiocarbon
abstract

The terrestrial sediment archives of northern Germany provide important insights into Late Pleistocene climate variability, but continuous and well‐dated records remain rare. In particular, the age and stratigraphic position of the Oerel and Glinde interstadials within the Early Weichselian to pleniglacial transition remain poorly constrained. These interstadials are commonly attributed to MIS 3, but this assignment is uncertain due to limited numerical dating, reliance on biostratigraphic correlation and the limitations of radiocarbon dating near its upper range. This study presents a new, high‐resolution multi‐proxy record (sedimentology, geochemistry, palynology) from two sediment cores at Lichtenberg, Lower Saxony, positioned directly next to a multi‐phased Middle Palaeolithic archaeological site and supported by a robust chronology based on new optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) ages. The record documents the vegetation and landscape dynamics from the late Eemian interglacial into the Weichselian pleniglacial, capturing major climatic transitions and the onset of cold, periglacial conditions. Within this sequence, we identify two previously unresolved climatic oscillations (Dangenstorf I and II), characterised by expansions of Betula nana ‐shrub tundra and increased organic accumulation. Based on pollen assemblages and OSL dating, these oscillations are correlated with the Oerel and Glinde interstadials, which are themselves stratigraphically positioned between the Odderade interstadial and the onset of the Weichselian pleniglacial. Furthermore, our OSL chronology places them firmly within the MIS 5a/4 transition. We assign them to the Greenland interstadials (GI) 20 and 19.2, respectively, rather than to MIS 3 as previously assumed. The Lichtenberg record thus provides a continuous, well‐dated archive that redefines the chronostratigraphy of the Early Weichselian to pleniglacial transition in northern Germany and strengthens its correlation with North Atlantic climate variability.

Isolated Northern Hemisphere Cooling in an Idealized Model Promotes Ocean Carbon Storage and Glacial-like Ocean Features

Journal of Climate · 2026-10-22
25.0 matched: ice sheet, NH ice sheets, sea ice, ocean circulation, Southern Ocean, interglacial, glacial cycle, insolation, climate feedback, climate change
abstract

Abstract Earth’s past glacial cycles are paced by orbital insolation changes, but internal climate feedbacks modulating atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO 2 ) are necessary to explain the full magnitude of glacial–interglacial climate variability. Identifying such feedbacks and linking them to orbitally paced forcing remains a challenge, impeding a comprehensive understanding of the glacial cycles. Orbital variations, determining the latitudinal distribution and seasonality of insolation, are primarily important for allowing the buildup of large Northern Hemisphere ice sheets. Meanwhile, the processes canonically understood to drive CO 2 drawdown and mediate ocean–atmosphere carbon partitioning are largely hosted in the far-flung Southern Ocean. This study explores the role of remote Northern Hemisphere temperature forcing in influencing Southern Ocean conditions that impact global ocean circulation and carbon content. In a set of idealized ocean–sea ice model simulations, we find that cooling air temperatures to glacial-like levels at the high northern latitudes (emulating the thermal effect of a large Northern Hemisphere ice sheet) drives a substantial CO 2 drawdown of 32 ppm—or 74% the magnitude of a simulation forced by globally glacial-like temperatures everywhere (43 ppm). Northern cooling is found to cool not only northern-sourced middepth waters but ultimately also global deep waters, resulting in a strong solubility-driven CO 2 drawdown component. It also drives several characteristic features of glacial periods, including expanded southern sea ice and a colder, saltier, and better stratified deep ocean, which contributes to air–sea CO 2 disequilibrium. These results demonstrate an interhemispheric connection between Northern Hemisphere conditions paced by orbital variations and Southern Hemisphere processes that modulate deep-ocean carbon content. Significance Statement Linking the orbital pacing of the ice ages—primarily expressed by the growth and decay of Northern Hemisphere ice sheets—to processes hosted in the Southern Ocean that can regulate ocean carbon storage has long posed a challenge. Here, we demonstrate using idealized simulations a clear influence of Northern Hemisphere cooling on Southern Ocean conditions and global ocean circulation and carbon content, which in turn draws down carbon dioxide (CO 2 ) from the atmosphere. This result underscores the importance of conditions in the high northern latitudes in shaping the ocean–atmosphere carbon balance, offering lessons about the interconnectedness of remote regions that are relevant to both past and contemporary climate change.

abstract

Abstract Global glacier models typically rely on temperature-index mass balance calculations forced with aggregated meteorological data, limiting their ability to resolve short-term variability. Here, we develop a daily-resolution version of the Global Glacier Evolution Model (GloGEM), which we apply globally and compare against the monthly configuration. Our results show that the daily model more closely reproduces mass balance observations and provides a more detailed representation of glacier runoff. Accounting for short-term meteorological variability leads to systematically greater projected mass loss, with up to 25% greater loss in Svalbard. These differences arise from extreme melt days, precipitation-phase transitions, and surface-type feedbacks not fully represented at monthly resolution. Resolving high-frequency forcing, or accounting for its effects through parameterisations, is therefore essential for robust glacier projections, while its omission may systematically underestimate future glacier loss.

Ocean carbon sequestration during glacial climates due to combined Antarctic sea ice expansion and ocean circulation change

Sandy Grégorio, Jonathan Maitland Lauderdale, Louis-Philippe Nadeau, Raffaele Ferrari
Science Advances · 2026-10-21
22.0 matched: Antarctic, sea ice, ocean circulation, Southern Ocean, interglacial, Quaternary
abstract

Glacial-interglacial transitions punctuate Earth’s Quaternary climate, accompanied by substantial atmospheric carbon dioxide and ocean carbon storage changes. The cause of atmospheric carbon drawdown during glaciations is uncertain, involving many mechanisms whose interactions are poorly understood. Here, we show that the combined expansion of Antarctic sea ice and associated changes in the meridional overturning efficiently sequester carbon in the ocean abyss, leading to a decline in atmospheric carbon dioxide. In contrast, changes in sea ice and circulation individually result in ocean carbon dioxide outgassing. When sea ice expansion and circulation changes act together, they enhance biological carbon storage by reducing ocean-atmosphere fluxes in the Southern Ocean, without invoking external drivers of the biological pump. This reduction inhibits the outgassing of upwelled remineralized carbon and promotes its resubduction into the abyss. Together, these results reveal a reinforcing feedback linking atmospheric cooling, Antarctic sea ice expansion, and ocean circulation changes to enhanced biological carbon storage and atmospheric carbon dioxide drawdown.

abstract

Glaciovolcanic products are important proxies for paleoenvironmental reconstructions in volcanic regions, especially when these products can be dated. A lava-fed delta sequence exposed 400 to 800 m asl on the flanks of Katla volcano, south Iceland, erupted and was emplaced into a past englacial lake. A rising passage zone between subaqueous and subaerial lithofacies indicates a dynamic lake surface level during delta progradation with a final elevation ~790 m asl. The glacier surface surrounding the lake is estimated to have been ~900 m asl, and the glacier was smaller than the Younger Dryas maximum. The lava ages also provide estimates of the incision rates of canyons that have dissected the lava-fed delta from the flank of Katla volcano. Formation of ~200 m-deep canyons since the emplacement of the upper lavas indicate rapid incision enhanced by episodic outburst flooding associated with subsequent eruptions and destabilisation of the glacier with climate amelioration. Thus, careful consideration of landscape modification is crucial when reconstructing the evolution of glaciovolcanic areas.

abstract

ABSTRACT Many scientific fields compare two or more noisy time series that integrate the same process but are recorded on different time scales. In paleoclimate studies, for example, proxy measurements are collected versus stratigraphic depth in a climate archive and then converted to calendar time. Synchronizing two proxy records often requires estimating an alignment that maps the depth (or preliminary age) of an input record onto the calendar‐time scale of an absolutely‐dated target record so that corresponding proxy signals line up. Existing alignment approaches are generally optimization‐based and return a single transformation, providing limited formal uncertainty quantification. Here, we introduce BSync, a Bayesian synchronization framework that treats alignments as inference over a monotone time‐mapping function to match an input to a target record. The alignment is expressed as a transformation of the input depth (or age) scale to match the target record, achieved through a link function that locally expands and compresses the input scale. BSync jointly infers the aligned chronology and provides posterior uncertainty for the time‐warping function and the resulting age scale. abstract

Abstract Both canonical theory and climate model simulations predict an increase in globally averaged precipitation with radiatively-forced warming. However, terrestrial processes control surface water and energy fluxes, and therefore can impact surface temperatures independently of radiative forcing. Here, we investigate the sensitivity of the global hydrological cycle to warming induced by changes in land surface conditions using perturbed parameter ensembles from two climate models, which sample a broad range of evapotranspiration (ET) states through modifications to land model parameters. The parameters are perturbed within the range of observationally constrained uncertainty and alter ET through different land surface pathways. Our results highlight that changes to land surface properties at the magnitude of parametric uncertainty influence warming and precipitation. ET-driven warming reduces land precipitation, opposite in sign to the precipitation increase from radiatively-forced warming. This response is not directly captured by scalings of the global hydrological cycle with radiative forcing. We therefore establish the potential significance of terrestrial ecosystem functioning, and its response to climate change, for hydroclimate.
