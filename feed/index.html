Environmental dynamics and timing of the late Eemian interglacial to the Weichselian pleniglacial at Lichtenberg, Germany: implications for the Oerel and Glinde interstadials

Alexis Badai Samudra, Michael Hein, Martin Theuerkauf, Neda Rahimzadeh, Marcel Weiss, David Colin Tanner, … (+1)
Boreas · 2026-10-19
35.0 matched: Greenland, interglacial, Eemian, MIS stage, proxy, sediment core, radiocarbon
abstract

The terrestrial sediment archives of northern Germany provide important insights into Late Pleistocene climate variability, but continuous and well‐dated records remain rare. In particular, the age and stratigraphic position of the Oerel and Glinde interstadials within the Early Weichselian to pleniglacial transition remain poorly constrained. These interstadials are commonly attributed to MIS 3, but this assignment is uncertain due to limited numerical dating, reliance on biostratigraphic correlation and the limitations of radiocarbon dating near its upper range. This study presents a new, high‐resolution multi‐proxy record (sedimentology, geochemistry, palynology) from two sediment cores at Lichtenberg, Lower Saxony, positioned directly next to a multi‐phased Middle Palaeolithic archaeological site and supported by a robust chronology based on new optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) ages. The record documents the vegetation and landscape dynamics from the late Eemian interglacial into the Weichselian pleniglacial, capturing major climatic transitions and the onset of cold, periglacial conditions. Within this sequence, we identify two previously unresolved climatic oscillations (Dangenstorf I and II), characterised by expansions of Betula nana ‐shrub tundra and increased organic accumulation. Based on pollen assemblages and OSL dating, these oscillations are correlated with the Oerel and Glinde interstadials, which are themselves stratigraphically positioned between the Odderade interstadial and the onset of the Weichselian pleniglacial. Furthermore, our OSL chronology places them firmly within the MIS 5a/4 transition. We assign them to the Greenland interstadials (GI) 20 and 19.2, respectively, rather than to MIS 3 as previously assumed. The Lichtenberg record thus provides a continuous, well‐dated archive that redefines the chronostratigraphy of the Early Weichselian to pleniglacial transition in northern Germany and strengthens its correlation with North Atlantic climate variability.

Pteropod preservation reveals carbonate dissolution across the Mid-Pleistocene Transition and an Eemian preservation spike in the Andaman Sea

E. Sreevidya, R.A. Sebastian, M.B.L. Mascarenhas‐Pereira, B. Nagender Nath, R. Mohan, Adukkam V. Sijinkumar
Quaternary International · 2026-10-17
34.0 matched: interglacial, Eemian, Mid-Pleistocene transition, MIS stage, sediment core
abstract

Pteropods are aragonitic holoplanktonic molluscs whose shells preserved in marine sediments provide a sensitive archive of past seawater carbonate saturation state, intermediate-water ventilation, and monsoon-driven oceanographic change. Here, we present a ∼1.2 Myr record of pteropod assemblages and preservation from sediment core NGHP-17 (1344 m water depth) in the Andaman Sea. Sixteen species were quantified across 180 samples, together with the Fragmentation Ratio (FR), Limacina Dissolution Index (LDX), epipelagic/mesopelagic (Epi/Meso) ratio, and selected planktonic and benthic foraminiferal abundances. Pteropod preservation is episodic, with pronounced preservation peaks during several glacial intervals. In contrast, generally poor and intermittent preservation during MIS 25–14 characterizes much of the Mid-Pleistocene Transition (MPT), although preservation windows occur during MIS 22, 18, and 16. This pre-MBDI decline was followed by the Mid-Brunhes Dissolution Interval (MBDI; MIS 13–11), within which a pronounced MIS 12 preservation peak demonstrates that unfavorable conditions were not continuous. An earlier preservation event occurs during MIS 26. The highest pteropod abundance is observed across MIS 6–5e, with a maximum at ∼125 ka and species maxima spanning ∼137–116 ka, indicating a transient Termination II/deglacial-to-early-interglacial preservation event. The significant inverse relationship between total pteropod abundance and FR supports a substantial preservation control on the record. Overall, the results indicate that pteropod preservation at NGHP-17 was strongly influenced by changes in bottom-water aragonite preservation conditions, within a broader framework of large-scale carbonate-system changes and regional hydrographic variability.

abstract

ABSTRACT This study combines historical records, satellite remote sensing data, and inverse modelling to investigate crack formation and calving front evolution of the Fimbul Ice Shelf, Antarctica. Historical maps from the 1950s, together with satellite imagery from 1970s–2026, are used to reconstruct calving‐front positions and track the evolution of crevasses. Principal stress fields obtained from higher‐order three‐dimensional inverse ice‐flow modelling support the fracture analysis. The ice shelf is strongly controlled by ice rises of varying size, which act as pinning points and promote fracture formation. The observed fractures consist predominantly of mode I cracks and wing cracks generated by mode I fractures subjected to shear stresses. Our results indicate that the present‐day calving front of Trolltunga is a remnant of the last major calving event in 1967, subsequently modified by collisions with drifting icebergs. We identify crack formation associated with ice flow past ice rises, occurring both immediately downstream of grounded features and at distances of approximately 8–18 km upstream of the present calving front. A small grounded feature controls the shape of its eastern calving front, while the western margin is influenced by wing cracks developing within the western shear margin of Jutulstraumen and by mode I cracks originating near the Apollo Ice Rises. No significant change in fracture formation along the calving front is identified over the past five decades, providing no evidence of substantial present‐day structural change.

ABSTRACT While several marine and terrestrial sediment records provide the general climate boundary conditions for Iberia at a relatively low temporal resolution (often >100 years per sample) during the last several glacial cycles, few high‐resolution proxy records of continental climate have been developed from this region. Transient adjustment of the coupled ocean-atmosphere system to an abrupt change of sulphur dioxide emissions over North America and Europe

Buwen Dong, Rowan T Sutton, Jon I. Robson, W. K. Michael Lai
Journal of Climate · 2026-10-16
27.0 matched: abrupt change, Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, coupled climate model, ensemble
abstract

Abstract This study investigates the transient adjustment processes of the coupled ocean-atmosphere system to an abrupt change of sulphur dioxide emissions over North America and Europe by performing a set of ensemble simulations using a state-of-the art coupled climate model. The analysis focuses on the transient evolutions that allow an assessment of the sequence of cause and effect in circulation and thermal structure changes in the coupled ocean-atmosphere system. In response to an abrupt increase of sulphur dioxide emissions over North America and Europe, immediate changes in the atmosphere include reduced surface shortwave (SW) radiation and surface cooling in the northern hemisphere (NH), a southward displacement of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), and changes in the subtropical and subpolar jets in both hemispheres. In the ocean, the response of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) exhibits three timescales: 1) little AMOC change in the first 5 years, 2) rapid intensification of the AMOC, by about 2Sv, in years 6–15, and 3) a relatively steady AMOC state in years 16–30. We elucidate the distinct processes that govern these three stages. The large scale changes in global atmospheric circulation in the tropics and mid-latitudes are similar during the three stages of AMOC response, suggesting a weak feedback of AMOC changes on global atmospheric circulation. We conclude that a wider use of abrupt forcing experiments has great potential to further understanding of the impact of anthropogenic aerosols on the climate system.

Nd isotopes as a paleocirculation tracer: consideration of sedimentary context and assessment through a north-south Atlantic transect

25.0 matched: Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, ocean circulation, interglacial, glacial cycle, sediment core
abstract

Authigenic neodymium (Nd) isotope ratios have been extensively used over the past two decades to reconstruct past changes in the global ocean circulation. Nevertheless, their effectiveness as a water mass tracer is currently strongly disputed based on the potential for modification by non-conservative processes. This study compares authigenic Nd isotope records along a meridional transect in the Atlantic from sediment core sites chosen with careful consideration of sedimentary conditions that are conducive to preserving the original seawater signal, including high carbonate/low lithogenic detritus content, oxic conditions, and exclusion of sites near continental margins and regions impacted by local effects. Records over the last 1.5 Myr shows north-to-south patterns such that, with few exceptions, at each point in time, at nearly every location, Nd isotope ratios are lower toward the north, and higher toward the south. The same relationship is observed in seawater studies of the present-day Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) . Moreover, each site shows interglacial-glacial cyclicity, indicating systemic changes through time impacting the entire Atlantic basin. In addition, a comparison of Nd isotope records over the last glacial cycle reaffirms these same meridional trends at high temporal resolution. The coherence of the present-day circulation dynamics and past meridional Nd isotope patterns provide strong evidence that such transects can be used to track temporal changes in the water mass structure and circulation in the Atlantic, if the deep-sea cores are carefully chosen for favorable conditions to preserve the original seawater Nd isotope signature.

Isolated Northern Hemisphere Cooling in an Idealized Model Promotes Ocean Carbon Storage and Glacial-like Ocean Features

Madison G. Shankle, Graeme A. MacGilchrist, Andrea Burke, Alice Marzocchi, Malte Friedrich Jansen, James W. B. Rae
Journal of Climate · 2026-10-22 new
25.0 matched: ice sheet, NH ice sheets, sea ice, ocean circulation, Southern Ocean, interglacial, glacial cycle, insolation, climate feedback, climate change
abstract

Abstract Earth’s past glacial cycles are paced by orbital insolation changes, but internal climate feedbacks modulating atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO 2 ) are necessary to explain the full magnitude of glacial–interglacial climate variability. Identifying such feedbacks and linking them to orbitally paced forcing remains a challenge, impeding a comprehensive understanding of the glacial cycles. Orbital variations, determining the latitudinal distribution and seasonality of insolation, are primarily important for allowing the buildup of large Northern Hemisphere ice sheets. Meanwhile, the processes canonically understood to drive CO 2 drawdown and mediate ocean–atmosphere carbon partitioning are largely hosted in the far-flung Southern Ocean. This study explores the role of remote Northern Hemisphere temperature forcing in influencing Southern Ocean conditions that impact global ocean circulation and carbon content. In a set of idealized ocean–sea ice model simulations, we find that cooling air temperatures to glacial-like levels at the high northern latitudes (emulating the thermal effect of a large Northern Hemisphere ice sheet) drives a substantial CO 2 drawdown of 32 ppm—or 74% the magnitude of a simulation forced by globally glacial-like temperatures everywhere (43 ppm). Northern cooling is found to cool not only northern-sourced middepth waters but ultimately also global deep waters, resulting in a strong solubility-driven CO 2 drawdown component. It also drives several characteristic features of glacial periods, including expanded southern sea ice and a colder, saltier, and better stratified deep ocean, which contributes to air–sea CO 2 disequilibrium. These results demonstrate an interhemispheric connection between Northern Hemisphere conditions paced by orbital variations and Southern Hemisphere processes that modulate deep-ocean carbon content. Significance Statement Linking the orbital pacing of the ice ages—primarily expressed by the growth and decay of Northern Hemisphere ice sheets—to processes hosted in the Southern Ocean that can regulate ocean carbon storage has long posed a challenge. Here, we demonstrate using idealized simulations a clear influence of Northern Hemisphere cooling on Southern Ocean conditions and global ocean circulation and carbon content, which in turn draws down carbon dioxide (CO 2 ) from the atmosphere. This result underscores the importance of conditions in the high northern latitudes in shaping the ocean–atmosphere carbon balance, offering lessons about the interconnectedness of remote regions that are relevant to both past and contemporary climate change.

abstract

Abstract Global glacier models typically rely on temperature-index mass balance calculations forced with aggregated meteorological data, limiting their ability to resolve short-term variability. Here, we develop a daily-resolution version of the Global Glacier Evolution Model (GloGEM), which we apply globally and compare against the monthly configuration. Our results show that the daily model more closely reproduces mass balance observations and provides a more detailed representation of glacier runoff. Accounting for short-term meteorological variability leads to systematically greater projected mass loss, with up to 25% greater loss in Svalbard. These differences arise from extreme melt days, precipitation-phase transitions, and surface-type feedbacks not fully represented at monthly resolution. Globally, the daily model yields ice volume losses of 26 ± 10% under low-emission and 47 ± 17% under high-emission scenarios over 2025-2100 (ensemble mean ± 1 StD), resulting in an additional 5-10% mass loss (20-33 mm of global mean sea-level rise) relative to the monthly implementation. Resolving high-frequency forcing, or accounting for its effects through parameterisations, is therefore essential for robust glacier projections, while its omission may systematically underestimate future glacier loss.

Ocean carbon sequestration during glacial climates due to combined Antarctic sea ice expansion and ocean circulation change

Sandy Grégorio, Jonathan Maitland Lauderdale, Louis-Philippe Nadeau, Raffaele Ferrari
Science Advances · 2026-10-21 new
22.0 matched: Antarctic, sea ice, ocean circulation, Southern Ocean, interglacial, Quaternary
abstract

Glacial-interglacial transitions punctuate Earth’s Quaternary climate, accompanied by substantial atmospheric carbon dioxide and ocean carbon storage changes. The cause of atmospheric carbon drawdown during glaciations is uncertain, involving many mechanisms whose interactions are poorly understood. Here, we show that the combined expansion of Antarctic sea ice and associated changes in the meridional overturning efficiently sequester carbon in the ocean abyss, leading to a decline in atmospheric carbon dioxide. In contrast, changes in sea ice and circulation individually result in ocean carbon dioxide outgassing. When sea ice expansion and circulation changes act together, they enhance biological carbon storage by reducing ocean-atmosphere fluxes in the Southern Ocean, without invoking external drivers of the biological pump. This reduction inhibits the outgassing of upwelled remineralized carbon and promotes its resubduction into the abyss. Together, these results reveal a reinforcing feedback linking atmospheric cooling, Antarctic sea ice expansion, and ocean circulation changes to enhanced biological carbon storage and atmospheric carbon dioxide drawdown.

We developed and tested a way to trace selected cloud particles in a simulation, including how they grow and merge. One run identifies particles that meet chosen conditions, and a second reconstructs only their saved histories. abstract

Green roofs can help cool hot cities, and the Weather Research and Forecasting model lets us test how much. We upgraded its multi-layer green-roof scheme, adding plant shading, realistic heat flow in wet soil, evaporation from soil and wet leaves, and water uptake from deeper soil.
