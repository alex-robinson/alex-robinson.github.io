Lead isotope evidence for Antarctic Ice Sheet changes in the Weddell Sea sector since the Last Glacial Maximum

Huang Huang, Yuanyang Hu, Marcus Gutjahr, Florian Kirsch, Lijuan Lu, Jianjun Zou, … (+4)
Boreas · 2026-10-28
48.0 matched: ice sheet, subglacial, Antarctic, Antarctica, polar, meltwater, ocean circulation, last glacial maximum, LGM, Holocene, sediment core, sea level
abstract

The Weddell Sea influences global ocean circulation and sea level through modulating Antarctic Bottom Water formation and Antarctic Ice Sheet discharges, but the history of ice‐sheet dynamics in this region remains poorly constrained due to limited well‐dated geological records. To provide a new geochemical perspective on ice‐sheet dynamics since the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM), this study presents paired authigenic and detrital Pb isotope records from sediment core PS1599‐3 recovered from the continental slope north of the Filchner Trough. Comparison with Antarctic Pb isotope provinces indicates that both detrital and authigenic Pb signatures were possibly sourced from a Shackleton Range‐like region in the south‐eastern Weddell Sea sector of East Antarctica. This suggests that dominant upstream drainage sources remained broadly stable throughout the last ~23 ka. We interpret the downcore Pb isotope variations to primarily reflect changes in the delivery of subglacial weathering‐derived Pb associated with grounding‐line dynamics in the Filchner Trough. Authigenic 206 Pb/ 204 Pb ratios show a sharp increase at ~23 ka, followed by two decreases at ~20 and 11–9 ka. We suggest that a re‐advance of grounded ice near the LGM was followed by initial retreat at ~20 ka and further grounding‐line retreat or reorganization during the Early Holocene. These findings highlight the use of authigenic Pb isotopes as potential tracers for reconstructing polar ice‐sheet dynamics and meltwater influences.

Cascading impacts of Atlantic meridional overturning circulation weakening and collapse on marine biogeochemistry

Paul E. Lerner, Anastasia Romanou
Communications Earth & Environment · 2026-11-04 · cites-me new
46.0 matched: Arctic, tipping point, bifurcation, Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, Earth system model, Earth system, ensemble
abstract

Abstract As anthropogenic emissions continue to increase Earth’s surface temperature, the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is at risk of weakening substantially and crossing a tipping point. Then, multiple Earth system components may be significantly and irreversibly altered. Here we investigate the responses of ocean carbon uptake, net primary productivity (NPP), oxygen concentration, and carbon storage to an AMOC collapse induced by extremes in the internal variability in the subpolar gyre under a moderate forcing scenario. While similar ensemble splitting has been found in other models, the NASA Goddard Institute for Space Studies (GISS) Earth system model (ESM) was the first to report an AMOC bifurcation due to internal variability, without the need to prescribe freshwater hosing. We find severe regional impacts, including collapsed NPP and deoxygenation in the subpolar North Atlantic, and reduced carbon uptake in the Arctic and subtropical North Atlantic. We identify the physical drivers of these ocean biogeochemical impacts and, for the first time, provide estimates of their lag and latency, i.e. the timescales over which these impacts begin to occur and are fully realized, respectively. Timescales are generally found to increase southward from the deep convection sites, but are remarkably similar between basins at similar latitudes.

The role of upper–deep ocean heat exchange in shaping warming patterns and climate feedbacks in CMIP6 models

37.0 matched: energy balance model, Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, Southern Ocean, millennial-scale, CMIP, climate feedback, CO2, ensemble
abstract

Abstract Surface warming patterns modulate climate feedbacks. Ocean heat exchange redistributes heat between the upper and deep ocean, shaping warming patterns. However, its influence on climate feedbacks remains unclear. We explore this issue in the CMIP6 abrupt-4×CO2 experiment by dividing the simulation into fast, intermediate and slow phases and using an effective upper–deep ocean heat exchange coefficient (γ) inferred from a two-layer energy balance model. The intermediate phase stands out as the period when ocean heat exchange leaves its strongest imprint on warming patterns and climate feedbacks. Models with stronger ocean heat exchange (high-γ ensemble) differ from those with weaker exchange (low-γ ensemble) in both background ocean state and response, exhibiting weaker mean-state stratification and a stronger Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) favoring stronger heat penetration, subsurface warming, and distinct warming pattern and feedback evolution. Across the three phases, the low-γ ensemble sustains intensified warming in the tropical eastern Pacific and Southern Ocean alongside subdued warming in the Northern Hemisphere extratropics. However, the high-γ ensemble experiences a warming pattern reversal over these regions, tied to stronger intermediate-phase heat penetration and AMOC weakening. This centennial-scale warming pattern reversal, overlooked in previous work, partly mirrors the millennial-scale reversal previously found in multi-millennial abrupt-4×CO2 simulations. The high-γ ensemble also exhibits a larger increase and later peak change in net climate feedback. These findings highlight ocean heat exchange and associated ocean mean-state differences as key controls on time-evolving warming patterns and climate feedbacks.

Abstract. The vast majority of studies examining the impact of freshwater from ice sheet melting on the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) use climate models that cannot resolve mesoscale ocean processes and do not include an accurate spatio-temporal distribution of the freshwater forcing. These two factors critically affect the nature of the AMOC response. Science Advances · 2026-11-04 new
28.0 matched: hysteresis, Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, interglacial, millennial-scale, sediment core, sea level
abstract

The stability of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is a major uncertainty in projections of future climate. However, direct evidence for AMOC tipping behavior on orbital timescales remains scarce. Here, we reconstruct the strength of Iceland-Scotland Overflow Water, a key lower branch of the deep Atlantic circulation, over the past 800,000 years by combining 12 current-speed records from North Atlantic sediment cores with a data-driven hybrid model. The resulting reconstruction reveals persistent alternation between weak (glacial) and strong (interglacial) circulation modes, pervasive millennial-scale variability within both regimes, and systematic asymmetry between weak-to-strong recoveries and strong-to-weak collapses. Threshold and phase-space analyses show that regime shifts occur within a narrow window of atmospheric carbon dioxide and sea level and often follow hysteresis-like loops. Although bistability persists throughout the last 800,000 years, its sensitivity and timing evolve through time. These findings provide long-term, high-resolution, data-driven constraints on the bistability of Nordic Seas overflow waters and its implications for AMOC resilience in a warming world.

abstract

Abstract Paleoenvironmental reconstructions using sediments from isolation basins including lakes are useful approaches to obtain evidence of sea-level and ice-sheet changes in Antarctica. However, a detailed study using multiple sediment cores of how isolation basins respond to external forcings is needed to improve the accuracy of paleoenvironmental reconstructions. We reconstructed how the water mass structure of Lake Nurume, located in Langhovde, Lützow-Holm Bay, East Antarctica, changed in response to sea-level and ice-sheet changes during the Holocene using sediments from different water depths and nearby trenches. Sedimentary observations, diatom analyses, and X-ray fluorescence core scanner analyses were conducted to reconstruct the three-dimensional characterization of water mass structure combined with the paleotopographic reconstruction using glacial isostatic adjustment modelling. Finally, at approximately 0.5 cal kyr BP, the lake became completely isolated from the sea, establishing its current water mass structure. These findings contribute to more accurate paleoenvironmental reconstructions such as sea-level and ice-sheet changes using sediments from isolation basins in Antarctica.

abstract

Abstract The timing and duration of monsoon rainfall shape regional hydroclimate, yet their response to abrupt climate change remains poorly constrained. Here, we use 25-km-resolution climate simulations to investigate the seasonal migration of the East Asian summer monsoon rain belt during Heinrich Stadials, periods associated with collapsed Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC). We find that the rain belt migrated northward 18 days later than during interstadials. This delay kept the monsoon front longer over central China, prolonging early-summer rainfall despite an overall weaker monsoon. Our results suggest that abrupt Atlantic changes can reshape both the amount and seasonal timing of East Asian summer rainfall. These findings offer a palaeoclimate perspective on potential changes in monsoon seasonality under future AMOC weakening.

abstract

The southern Levant extends between the hyperarid Sahara–Arabia Desert belt in the south and the temperate Mediterranean climate zone in the north, making the regional hydroclimate extremely sensitive to even small perturbations of global climate. The region further served as a major pathway for the “out-of-Africa” dispersal of fauna, flora, and hominins throughout the Quaternary, as well as for human cultural development from the Paleolithic to the Neolithic. Spanning the length of the Levant is the Dead Sea Transform (DST) that transfers the opening at the Red Sea in the south to the collision at the Taurus Mountains in the north. Pull-apart basins along the DST host the hypersaline Dead Sea and freshwater Lake Kinneret, whose sedimentary records document the regional hydroclimate and seismic history. Lake Kinneret (a.k.a. the Sea of Galilee) is the largest freshwater lake in the Levant, whose precursors have deposited a thick sequence of lacustrine sediments since the late Miocene. Seismic profiles across the tectonically subsiding basin in the middle of the modern lake, as well as existing short sediment cores, indicate that the sedimentary sequences are generally bounded by two N–S-striking longitudinal faults and generally maintain stratigraphic order. The sedimentary column is dominated by primary authigenic calcite, whose chemical and isotopic composition documents regional hydrology and lacustrine conditions from at least the Pliocene. Accordingly, the sedimentary record of Lake Kinneret holds the potential to serve as one of the longest and most highly resolved lacustrine records since the Pliocene, yielding sub-centennial records of hydroclimate, paleoseismicity, geomagnetism, and paleobotanics in a region of fundamental importance for the global understanding of climate change, tectonics, and human evolution. The Lake KINneret Drilling (KIND) project aims to establish an exceptionally long Pliocene–Quaternary chronostratigraphic reference record that will contribute to a meridional synthesis of continental and marine core records along the East African Rift–Dead Sea Transform (EARDST) corridor. Owing to its location, the KIND record will yield transformative contributions to climate modeling and to our understanding of climate change in the eastern Mediterranean, a recognized global warming “hotspot”. Moreover, it will document seismic activity along the DST over long timescales, providing a basis for policymakers to prepare for this potential major hazard to infrastructure and human life. Taken together, the KIND project will provide unique and much-needed records from a tectonically active region that is projected to become increasingly arid over the coming century and that is already burdened by severe geopolitical strife. These records are critical for identifying and quantifying regional climate change patterns, assessing seismic risks, and evaluating the ecological impacts of these processes.

abstract

Northeast China and Russian Primorye lie at the confluence of the East Asian summer monsoon, Siberian High, and Pacific maritime circulation. The region experienced pronounced glacial-interglacial variability and served as a corridor for human dispersal among Siberia, East Asia, and the Americas. This review synthesises palaeoecological and archaeological evidence, principally from the past ca. 50 ka. Environmental records trace a transition from the cold, arid steppe of the Last Glacial Maximum (ca. 26.5–19.0 ka BP) to warmer, more forested Holocene landscapes, while revealing strong spatial heterogeneity and abrupt events. Although the ≥146 ka Harbin cranium predates the main review interval, it establishes a Middle Pleistocene hominin presence; securely dated archaeological sequences considered here begin in the Late Pleistocene. These records document developments from Upper Palaeolithic lithic industries to Neolithic sedentism, millet agriculture, and Bronze Age sociopolitical complexity. A central limitation is the spatial separation of most palaeoclimatic archives and archaeological sites. Their differing chronological resolution further restricts direct causal attribution, leaving many proposed climate-culture relationships as working hypotheses. We therefore advocate co-located multiproxy transects, improved chronological control, and tephrochronological correlation. A site-by-site appraisal of radiocarbon, OSL, and U-series chronologies identifies relationships that are currently testable. The Boisman-Krounovka sequence suggests that resource breadth and specialisation may mediate vulnerability to environmental change, although the roles of social organisation, mobility, and demography remain unresolved. Northeast Asia thus offers a framework for generating and testing hypotheses about climatic variability, demographic processes, and cultural change rather than demonstrating simple climate-driven causation.

Overshoot pathways: reversible biophysical change, irreversible socioeconomic impacts

Alaa Al Khourdajie, Marina Andrijevic, Edward A. Byers, Minal Pathak, Anna Pirani, Carl‐Friedrich Schleussner, … (+1)
Environmental Research Letters · 2026-08-07 · cites-me
17.0 matched: overshoot, irreversible, global warming
abstract

Abstract Exceeding global warming of 1.5 °C in the near term is now unavoidable. An overshoot pathway, in which exceedance is followed by decline to or below the threshold through net-negative emissions, is now the only remaining route back to 1.5 °C. Permanent exceedance forecloses the recovery from climate impacts that an overshoot pathway may permit, yet even an overshoot pathway leaves lasting legacies. To characterise the reversibility of temporary overshoot impacts, we propose a three-layer analytical framework distinguishing hazards (with four dimensions: magnitude, duration, rate of exceedance, and rate of decline), biophysical responses (reversible vs persistent change), and socioeconomic outcomes (reversible vs irreversible impact). We introduce the socioeconomic commitment threshold, endogenous to the overshoot trajectory: how much overshoot intensity a system can experience before the losses it accumulates persist after temperatures decline, explained by three mechanisms: non-substitutability, threshold-crossing, and lock-in. A typology linking biophysical persistence with socioeconomic irreversibility illustrates how reversible biophysical change can produce irreversible socioeconomic loss, while persistent biophysical change need not produce irreversible outcomes where adaptive capacity is sufficient. Whether temporary exceedance produces permanent harm is determined primarily by socioeconomic rather than biophysical factors, except where permanent biophysical change exceeds adaptation limits. For systems and communities with low adaptive capacity, overshoot concentrates irreversible legacies even where temperatures subsequently decline, placing equity and justice at the centre of overshoot assessment.
