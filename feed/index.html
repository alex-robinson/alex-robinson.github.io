Fracture and Calving Front Evolution: A Case Study of Fimbul Ice Shelf, Antarctica

Angelika Humbert, Lea‐Sophie Höyns, Sebastian Skatulla, Moreblessings Shoko, Patroba Achola Odera, Alex M. YUMBU, … (+4)
PAMM · 2026-10-16 new
33.0 matched: ice shelf, ice flow, Antarctica, calving, iceberg, remote sensing
abstract

ABSTRACT This study combines historical records, satellite remote sensing data, and inverse modelling to investigate crack formation and calving front evolution of the Fimbul Ice Shelf, Antarctica. Historical maps from the 1950s, together with satellite imagery from 1970s–2026, are used to reconstruct calving‐front positions and track the evolution of crevasses. Principal stress fields obtained from higher‐order three‐dimensional inverse ice‐flow modelling support the fracture analysis. The ice shelf is strongly controlled by ice rises of varying size, which act as pinning points and promote fracture formation. The observed fractures consist predominantly of mode I cracks and wing cracks generated by mode I fractures subjected to shear stresses. Our results indicate that the present‐day calving front of Trolltunga is a remnant of the last major calving event in 1967, subsequently modified by collisions with drifting icebergs. We identify crack formation associated with ice flow past ice rises, occurring both immediately downstream of grounded features and at distances of approximately 8–18 km upstream of the present calving front. Fractures initiated at these ice rises play a key role in shaping the lateral margins of Trolltunga. A small grounded feature controls the shape of its eastern calving front, while the western margin is influenced by wing cracks developing within the western shear margin of Jutulstraumen and by mode I cracks originating near the Apollo Ice Rises. No significant change in fracture formation along the calving front is identified over the past five decades, providing no evidence of substantial present‐day structural change.

abstract

Abstract Thwaites Glacier is a major contributor to Antarctic mass loss and a key source of uncertainty in future sea-level rise. Ocean-driven melting at its grounding line drives rapid retreat, yet most models treat the transition between grounded and floating ice as a sharp boundary, neglecting kilometer-scale seawater intrusions that develop as the grounding line migrates and forms a grounding zone. Here, we show that actively-melting grounding zones are required to reproduce nearly three decades of observed retreat, thinning, and acceleration of Thwaites Glacier in a calibrated ice-sheet model, with successful reconstruction occurring only when grounding zones are represented as 2-5 km wide regions. ABSTRACT While several marine and terrestrial sediment records provide the general climate boundary conditions for Iberia at a relatively low temporal resolution (often >100 years per sample) during the last several glacial cycles, few high‐resolution proxy records of continental climate have been developed from this region. Transient adjustment of the coupled ocean-atmosphere system to an abrupt change of sulphur dioxide emissions over North America and Europe

Buwen Dong, Rowan T Sutton, Jon I. Robson, W. K. Michael Lai
Journal of Climate · 2026-10-16 new
27.0 matched: abrupt change, Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, coupled climate model, ensemble
abstract

Abstract This study investigates the transient adjustment processes of the coupled ocean-atmosphere system to an abrupt change of sulphur dioxide emissions over North America and Europe by performing a set of ensemble simulations using a state-of-the art coupled climate model. The analysis focuses on the transient evolutions that allow an assessment of the sequence of cause and effect in circulation and thermal structure changes in the coupled ocean-atmosphere system. In response to an abrupt increase of sulphur dioxide emissions over North America and Europe, immediate changes in the atmosphere include reduced surface shortwave (SW) radiation and surface cooling in the northern hemisphere (NH), a southward displacement of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), and changes in the subtropical and subpolar jets in both hemispheres. In the ocean, the response of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) exhibits three timescales: 1) little AMOC change in the first 5 years, 2) rapid intensification of the AMOC, by about 2Sv, in years 6–15, and 3) a relatively steady AMOC state in years 16–30. We elucidate the distinct processes that govern these three stages. The large scale changes in global atmospheric circulation in the tropics and mid-latitudes are similar during the three stages of AMOC response, suggesting a weak feedback of AMOC changes on global atmospheric circulation. We conclude that a wider use of abrupt forcing experiments has great potential to further understanding of the impact of anthropogenic aerosols on the climate system.

abstract

Authigenic neodymium (Nd) isotope ratios have been extensively used over the past two decades to reconstruct past changes in the global ocean circulation. Nevertheless, their effectiveness as a water mass tracer is currently strongly disputed based on the potential for modification by non-conservative processes. This study compares authigenic Nd isotope records along a meridional transect in the Atlantic from sediment core sites chosen with careful consideration of sedimentary conditions that are conducive to preserving the original seawater signal, including high carbonate/low lithogenic detritus content, oxic conditions, and exclusion of sites near continental margins and regions impacted by local effects. Records over the last 1.5 Myr shows north-to-south patterns such that, with few exceptions, at each point in time, at nearly every location, Nd isotope ratios are lower toward the north, and higher toward the south. The same relationship is observed in seawater studies of the present-day Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) . Moreover, each site shows interglacial-glacial cyclicity, indicating systemic changes through time impacting the entire Atlantic basin. In addition, a comparison of Nd isotope records over the last glacial cycle reaffirms these same meridional trends at high temporal resolution. The coherence of the present-day circulation dynamics and past meridional Nd isotope patterns provide strong evidence that such transects can be used to track temporal changes in the water mass structure and circulation in the Atlantic, if the deep-sea cores are carefully chosen for favorable conditions to preserve the original seawater Nd isotope signature.

This study reconstructs hydroclimatic changes from the earlier Late Pleistocene to the mid-Holocene using the 3.88 m BT-1 trench profile in Bayan Tohom Gobi, southeastern Mongolia (45°06′50.6″ N, 112°31′47.1″ E; ∼970 m a.s.l.). Empirical constraints on ice cliff calving

Caroline T. Needell, Catherine Colello Walker, J. N. Bassis
Science · 2026-10-15
14.0 matched: ice sheet, calving, parameterization
abstract

When calving cliffs at the margins of Earth’s ice sheets exceed a critical height, runaway retreat may be initiated in a process called the marine ice cliff instability. However, sufficiently tall ice cliffs have rarely been observed. To address this gap, we measured the characteristics of 15,330 ice cliffs across Earth’s ice sheet margins using laser altimetry. We show that cliff height is highly correlated with mélange thickness, water depth, and crevassing. We found that tall cliffs calve more rapidly than parameterizations assume, but dynamic thinning and retreat into regions with low ice thickness gradients prevent runaway retreat. These observations suggest that model projections may underestimate subannual rates of cliff calving, but that the potential for instability will depend sensitively on additional quantities, not cliff height alone.

Perennially ice‐covered lake facies as proxies for ice cover properties and climate: Insights from Lake Joyce, Antarctica

Frances Rivera‐Hernández, Dawn Y. Sumner, Tyler Mackey
Sedimentology · 2026-10-16 new
14.0 matched: glacier, Antarctica, albedo, paleoclimate, proxy
abstract

ABSTRACT Process‐based depositional models for perennially ice‐covered lakes (PICLs) are important for interpreting palaeolake deposits and their connection to past climate. PICLs are only stable under specific climate states, and unlike other ice‐free or seasonally ice‐covered lakes, PICL deposits generally have bimodal grain sizes: mud introduced by streams and glaciers and moderately sorted medium to coarse sand deposited as disseminated grains, mounds and ridges from the ice cover. Previous work implies that ice properties influence the mud‐to‐sand ratio and sand depositional style. Here we present multi‐year observations from Lake Joyce, Antarctica, demonstrating how ice cover properties influence deposition to help inform interpretations of climate from the sedimentary record. Lake Joyce's ice cover had smooth to rough textures with rougher ice trapping more sediment and forming multi‐grain sediment pods, and smooth ice containing scattered grains. Low‐albedo medium sand grains preferentially melted into the ice over higher albedo grains and those that are finer and coarser. Thicker pods slowed underlying ice melt, whereas thinner pods accelerated it. Grains migrated downwards and accumulated at an equilibrium depth which was shallower than the ice cover thickness. Sediment pods commonly contained inverse grading, resulting from deflation and preferential melting of finer grains into the ice. These observations help inform PICL deposit interpretations: pods in rough ice result in sand mounds and ridges, while scattered grains on smooth ice lead to disseminated grains. Deposits have a narrow grain‐size range due to the preferential melting of medium sand, and inverse grading in mounds mirrors that of pods. Our observations suggest that ice roughness and thickness control where and how much sediment accumulates, its vertical migration through the ice and depositional style. Temporal or spatial variations in ice properties may be preserved as stratigraphic or lateral facies changes. Because ice properties are climate‐dependent, stratigraphic variations in PICL facies may be used as palaeoclimate indicators if found on Earth and Mars.
