Cascading impacts of Atlantic meridional overturning circulation weakening and collapse on marine biogeochemistry

Paul E. Lerner, Anastasia Romanou
Communications Earth & Environment · 2026-11-04 · cites-me
46.0 matched: Arctic, tipping point, bifurcation, Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, Earth system model, Earth system, ensemble
abstract

Abstract As anthropogenic emissions continue to increase Earth’s surface temperature, the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is at risk of weakening substantially and crossing a tipping point. Then, multiple Earth system components may be significantly and irreversibly altered. Here we investigate the responses of ocean carbon uptake, net primary productivity (NPP), oxygen concentration, and carbon storage to an AMOC collapse induced by extremes in the internal variability in the subpolar gyre under a moderate forcing scenario. While similar ensemble splitting has been found in other models, the NASA Goddard Institute for Space Studies (GISS) Earth system model (ESM) was the first to report an AMOC bifurcation due to internal variability, without the need to prescribe freshwater hosing. We find severe regional impacts, including collapsed NPP and deoxygenation in the subpolar North Atlantic, and reduced carbon uptake in the Arctic and subtropical North Atlantic. We identify the physical drivers of these ocean biogeochemical impacts and, for the first time, provide estimates of their lag and latency, i.e. the timescales over which these impacts begin to occur and are fully realized, respectively. Timescales are generally found to increase southward from the deep convection sites, but are remarkably similar between basins at similar latitudes.

Evaluating ice shelf basal melt rates in the Southern Ocean high-resolution model against satellite remote sensing

Andy Dinh, Eric J. Rignot, Matthew R. Mazloff, Ian G. Fenty
Frontiers in Marine Science · 2026-11-06
40.0 matched: ice sheet, ice shelf, grounding line, Antarctica, basal melt, Southern Ocean, sea level, sea-level rise, remote sensing
abstract

Ice shelf melt rates by a warm ocean have a critical impact on ice sheet mass balance in Antarctica and in turn on the rates of sea level rise. Validating and tuning modeled ice shelf melt rates is a challenge. Here we discuss the issues by presenting validation of melt rates calculated from the Southern Ocean high-resolution model (SOhi) over the year 2007. SOhi includes full physics, tides, and an updated bathymetric compilation. We compare the model results to two satellite-derived estimates. Overall, we find that the model overestimates ice shelf melt, which we attribute to the ocean model initial (warm) conditions in some areas, bathymetric uncertainties in others, simplified physics on some ice shelves, and also to the inherent limitations of the satellite-derived estimates. The SOhi model nevertheless provides a comprehensive, detailed mapping of ice shelf melt rates around Antarctica; it captures higher melt near grounding lines and ice fronts, areas of freezing versus areas of high melt water production. The identified discrepancies between model and satellite-estimates provide guidelines for model improvements, requirements for additional observations of ice shelf cavities, and assembling of calibration/validation datasets to constrain the models.

abstract

Earth’s climate oscillations between interglacial and glacial periods have driven significant sea level changes. Sri Lanka, located in the Indian Ocean, provides an ideal natural setting for studying paleo sea level changes. This study compiles 92 Holocene sea level records and identifies last interglacial stratigraphic evidence from Point Kudrimalai to reconstruct paleo sea level fluctuations in Sri Lanka. In addition, this study evaluates underutilized sea level indicators such as beachrock, coastal barrier sands, and mangrove swamps to address methodological challenges in reconstructing past sea levels. Journal of Climate · 2026-11-05
37.0 matched: Greenland, Arctic, Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, coupled climate model, model intercomparison, CMIP, radiative forcing, ensemble
abstract

Abstract Growing observational and modeling evidence suggests that the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) has weakened since the 1980s, with reductions in North American and European (NAEU) aerosols identified as a potential contributing factor. Here we use fully coupled climate model experiments to demonstrate that the radiative forcing associated with decreased NAEU aerosols weakens the AMOC by enhancing freshwater transport from the Arctic and subtropical regions into the deep convection region. Reduced aerosol loading induces large-scale atmospheric circulation adjustments, including cyclonic wind stress anomalies over the Arctic Ocean and the subtropical North Atlantic. Over the Arctic Ocean, the cyclonic wind stress anomaly weakens the Beaufort Gyre, releasing freshwater that accumulates north of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago and Greenland. This freshwater is then exported through Baffin Bay into the Labrador Sea, suppressing deep convection there. Simultaneously, enhanced cyclonic wind stress over the subtropical North Atlantic weakens the North Atlantic Current (NAC), reducing the northward transport of saline water and promoting the freshwater accumulation within the subpolar gyre. This freshwater anomaly is then advected toward the Labrador Sea, further suppressing deep convection and weakening the AMOC. Together, our results identify a remotely driven pathway through which reductions in NAEU aerosols weaken the AMOC via wind-driven freshwater advection. The robustness of this mechanism is further supported by the multi-model ensemble mean from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) simulations, which exhibits consistent oceanic and atmospheric circulation responses.

abstract

Abstract. The vast majority of studies examining the impact of freshwater from ice sheet melting on the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) use climate models that cannot resolve mesoscale ocean processes and do not include an accurate spatio-temporal distribution of the freshwater forcing. These two factors critically affect the nature of the AMOC response. Our study fills that gap with a set of three hosing experiments performed with the global configurations of the eddy-rich climate model EC-Earth3P-VHR. The model is forced for 21 years with a spatial and monthly distribution of Greenland meltwater fluxes derived from observations, equal to 0.04 Sv on an annual average. Within the first year, we observe a response of reduced salinity in the Greenland and Labrador currents. This is accompanied by an acceleration and a cooling along the currents that lead to a rapid weakening of the AMOC at subpolar latitudes. Around year 7, deep mixing in the Labrador Sea begins to weaken due to as freshwater anomalies accumulate through lateral exchanges with the boundary currents. This shallowing of the mixed layer further weakens the AMOC, resulting in a stronger reduction that reaches also the subtropical latitudes. By the end of the simulation, the AMOC has weakened by almost 3 Sv at subppolar latitudes (i.e. a decrease of around 20 %), with an average relative decrease of 10 % for the whole Northern Hemisphere. The reduction in the AMOC is strong enough for some global climate impacts to emerge, such as the “bipolar seesaw” temperature response.

Persistent bistability of Nordic Seas overflow waters over 800,000 years

Nathan Stevenard, Aline Govin, Catherine Kissel, Didier Swingedouw, Samuel Toucanne, Nathaëlle Bouttes
Science Advances · 2026-11-04
28.0 matched: hysteresis, Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, AMOC, overturning circulation, interglacial, millennial-scale, sediment core, sea level
abstract

The stability of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is a major uncertainty in projections of future climate. However, direct evidence for AMOC tipping behavior on orbital timescales remains scarce. Here, we reconstruct the strength of Iceland-Scotland Overflow Water, a key lower branch of the deep Atlantic circulation, over the past 800,000 years by combining 12 current-speed records from North Atlantic sediment cores with a data-driven hybrid model. The resulting reconstruction reveals persistent alternation between weak (glacial) and strong (interglacial) circulation modes, pervasive millennial-scale variability within both regimes, and systematic asymmetry between weak-to-strong recoveries and strong-to-weak collapses. Threshold and phase-space analyses show that regime shifts occur within a narrow window of atmospheric carbon dioxide and sea level and often follow hysteresis-like loops. Although bistability persists throughout the last 800,000 years, its sensitivity and timing evolve through time. These findings provide long-term, high-resolution, data-driven constraints on the bistability of Nordic Seas overflow waters and its implications for AMOC resilience in a warming world.

abstract

Dansgaard-Oeschger (DO) events during the last ice age are thought to reflect rapid shifts in the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC). Greenland ice cores suggest warming transitions within only a few decades—or even just over 10 years. Using precisely dated speleothem oxygen-isotope records from the Asian monsoon region, we have shown that the same DO warmings unfolded over more than a century. abstract

ABSTRACT Understanding Earth‐surface processes along the fringes of large ice sheets is integral to deciphering cold‐region environmental variability and anticipating slope instability under climate change. We investigate the Meseta Chile Chico, a volcanic plateau in the central Patagonian Andes, located at the eastern margin of the former Patagonian Ice Sheet. Here, glacier retreat patterns are well‐constrained, while the influence of periglacial, paraglacial, and postglacial processes is not. The geomorphology of the Meseta Chile Chico reflects an interplay between inherited geology, (de)glaciation, and climate, highlighting the importance of an interdisciplinary approach for understanding recent landscape dynamics in deglaciating mountain regions.

Abstract The timing and duration of monsoon rainfall shape regional hydroclimate, yet their response to abrupt climate change remains poorly constrained. Here, we use 25-km-resolution climate simulations to investigate the seasonal migration of the East Asian summer monsoon rain belt during Heinrich Stadials, periods associated with collapsed Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC). Overshoot pathways: reversible biophysical change, irreversible socioeconomic impacts

Alaa Al Khourdajie, Marina Andrijevic, Edward A. Byers, Minal Pathak, Anna Pirani, Carl‐Friedrich Schleussner, … (+1)
Environmental Research Letters · 2026-08-07 · cites-me
17.0 matched: overshoot, irreversible, global warming
abstract

Abstract Exceeding global warming of 1.5 °C in the near term is now unavoidable. An overshoot pathway, in which exceedance is followed by decline to or below the threshold through net-negative emissions, is now the only remaining route back to 1.5 °C. Permanent exceedance forecloses the recovery from climate impacts that an overshoot pathway may permit, yet even an overshoot pathway leaves lasting legacies. To characterise the reversibility of temporary overshoot impacts, we propose a three-layer analytical framework distinguishing hazards (with four dimensions: magnitude, duration, rate of exceedance, and rate of decline), biophysical responses (reversible vs persistent change), and socioeconomic outcomes (reversible vs irreversible impact). We introduce the socioeconomic commitment threshold, endogenous to the overshoot trajectory: how much overshoot intensity a system can experience before the losses it accumulates persist after temperatures decline, explained by three mechanisms: non-substitutability, threshold-crossing, and lock-in. A typology linking biophysical persistence with socioeconomic irreversibility illustrates how reversible biophysical change can produce irreversible socioeconomic loss, while persistent biophysical change need not produce irreversible outcomes where adaptive capacity is sufficient. Whether temporary exceedance produces permanent harm is determined primarily by socioeconomic rather than biophysical factors, except where permanent biophysical change exceeds adaptation limits. For systems and communities with low adaptive capacity, overshoot concentrates irreversible legacies even where temperatures subsequently decline, placing equity and justice at the centre of overshoot assessment.

Abstract Large‐scale, high‐resolution estimation of snow water equivalent (SWE) in mountainous regions remains challenging.
